## Heavy and expensive is not the same as high security

A solid brass deadbolt that weighs a pound and costs a lot can still open to a cheap copied key. Weight, finish and price tell you nothing about what is going on inside the cylinder, which is where most of the real security lives.

A true high security deadbolt is defined by test results and key rules, not by feel. When we assess a lock, we look at three separate layers, and a weak link in any one of them undoes the other two.

| Layer | What it protects against | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Hardware | Bolt and housing failing under force or heavy use | ANSI/BHMA A156.36 Grade 1, or A156.40 AAA on residential models |
| Cylinder | Picking, bumping, drilling and forcing the keyway | A UL 437 listing for the exact cylinder; BHMA's A156.30 covers high security cylinders |
| Keys | Unauthorized copies by anyone who borrows a key | A restricted or patented keyway with documented authorization |

## Layer one: hardware that survives force and wear

The bolt, housing and mounting have to hold before the cylinder even matters. BHMA's public summary of A156.36 says Grade 1 deadlocks must pass 250,000 cycles and seven security tests, one of which puts 1,350 lb of force on the door against the bolt.

Residential deadbolts can instead carry A156.40 letter ratings, where AAA is the top rating for security, durability and finish. Some models carry both. How those two scales differ is a topic of its own, covered in our [guide to deadbolt grades](/deadbolt-guides/deadbolt-grades-ansi-bhma/).

Here is the trap: a Grade 1 or AAA rating describes the hardware, not necessarily the cylinder or the key. Schlage's B600 is a Grade 1 commercial deadbolt, but it is UL 437 listed only when fitted with a Primus UL 437 cylinder. Order it with a standard cylinder and you have strong hardware with an ordinary keyway.

## Layer two: a cylinder tested against picking and drilling

UL 437 is the listing most people mean when they say high security. According to a distributor's summary of the standard, it tests a cylinder against picking, drilling and forced entry, such as a tool driven into the keyway and turned, for a minimum set time under lab conditions.

Deadbolt Fixers does not quote attack times. The standard is paywalled, and the numbers repeated online do not agree with each other. What matters to you is whether the specific cylinder on your door is listed.

### What resistance looks like inside the cylinder

Manufacturers get there in different ways. Schlage describes its Primus cylinders as using hardened steel and anti-drill pins, plus a second, independent sidebar and finger-pin mechanism that protects against picking and bumping. Mul-T-Lock lists UL 437 and ANSI Grade 1 conformance for its Hercular deadbolt, which has a standard 1" bolt throw.

<Callout type="warning" title="Check the cylinder, not the brand">
Brands often sell high security and standard versions of very similar-looking locks, and the same deadbolt body may be offered with several cylinder options. Before you pay a high security price, confirm the UL 437 listing applies to the exact cylinder in the box, not just the product family.
</Callout>

## Layer three: key control and restricted keyways

A pick-resistant cylinder does little if anyone who borrows your key for an afternoon can get a copy cut. Key control closes that gap. On a restricted or patented keyway, blanks are not sold openly, so copies come only through authorized channels.

Schlage says its Primus keys are tightly controlled, with factory-milled side bitting designed to make unauthorized duplication hard. Its Everest 29 system is patent protected through 2029, and the company states those keys cannot be duplicated without authorization.

Key control is also a habit. Schlage's Everest 29 manual advises a written policy naming who may authorize new keys. For a household, that can be as simple as one person holding the authorization and a short list of who has keys. For a business with staff turnover, our page on [deadbolts for storefronts and offices](/commercial-deadbolt-locks/) goes further into managing keys across a team.

A restricted keyway also changes servicing. A standard cylinder can be rekeyed at almost any locksmith shop, but a controlled system usually needs authorized parts, so it is worth knowing who can [rekey the lock later](/deadbolt-rekeying/) before you commit to a system.

## Doors that justify a high security deadbolt

Not every door needs this level of hardware. These are the situations where we recommend it.

<Checklist title="A high security deadbolt is worth it when" items={[
  'Keys have been lost, or copies may be circulating that you cannot account for',
  'Many people need keys: tenants, staff, cleaners, contractors, family members',
  'The door protects high-value equipment, inventory, records or a home office',
  'The door is out of sight from the street, giving someone time to work on the lock undisturbed',
  'You already have a strong door, frame and strike, and the cylinder is now the weakest point',
]} />

The last item matters. A high security deadbolt on a hollow door with a strike held by short screws spends money in the wrong place. BHMA itself notes that lab results can differ in the field depending on installation, door and frame construction.

## The rest of the door has to keep up

Pick and drill resistance protect the cylinder. They do nothing for the jamb. A kick-in attacks the strike, so every high security deadbolt we fit gets checked against [a reinforced strike plate with long screws](/deadbolt-strike-plate-reinforcement/), and we recommend that upgrade wherever the existing strike is thin or poorly anchored.

The same logic applies to smart locks. Consumer Reports notes that a retrofit smart lock's kick, pick and drill resistance depends entirely on the deadbolt it is paired with. If you want app control and serious resistance, the deadbolt underneath has to be the high security part. Our [smart deadbolt installation](/smart-deadbolt-installation/) page covers which approach fits which door.

## Field tip: look the lock up before you buy it

Write down the exact model number, not just the brand, and search it in BHMA's free Certified Products Directory to confirm the grade. Then check the manufacturer's spec sheet for the words "UL 437 listed" next to the cylinder you are ordering.

Finally, ask the seller one question: who can authorize a copy of this key? If the answer is "anyone with the blank," you are not buying a high security deadbolt, whatever the packaging says.
