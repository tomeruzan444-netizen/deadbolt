## The door that usually prompts the call

A typical request starts with a front door that has a tall glass sidelight a few inches from the deadbolt. The owner has noticed that anyone willing to break one pane could reach in and turn the thumb turn, and the lock would open as if they had a key.

A double cylinder deadbolt replaces that thumb turn with a second keyed cylinder. With no turn piece on the inside, broken glass stops being a way to work the bolt. The trade is that everyone inside now needs a key to get out, including in a fire.

That trade is why our double cylinder deadbolt installation starts with questions about the door and the people behind it, not with the lock. If you are still weighing the two designs, our [single vs double cylinder comparison](/deadbolt-guides/single-vs-double-cylinder-deadbolt/) covers the pros and cons household by household. This page is about doing the job properly once a keyed interior looks like the right answer.

## Doors where a keyed interior earns its place

A double cylinder lock is a fix for one specific weakness. It is worth considering when most of these are true:

- **Glass is within reach of the lock.** That can be a glass panel in the door itself, a sidelight, or a window next to the frame.
- **The door is not the home's required exit.** Most homes have another exterior door that can stay on a thumb turn (see the code section below).
- **Everyone who sleeps in the house can find and use the inside key** quickly, in the dark, without help.
- **The household will keep the key in one spot,** every time the bolt is thrown from inside.

If the glass is well away from the lock, or this is the only way out, skip the second cylinder. A single cylinder deadbolt paired with [a reinforced strike plate and long screws](/deadbolt-strike-plate-reinforcement/) answers the more common attack, a kick at the jamb. Consumer Reports found that swapping a stock strike for a box strike with 3-inch screws significantly improved kick-in resistance for most locks it tested.

## Egress rules that shape double cylinder deadbolt installation

Residential codes based on the International Residential Code cover this in section R311.2. The 2009 model IRC wording, as widely quoted, reads: "Egress doors shall be readily openable from inside the dwelling without the use of a key or special knowledge or effort."

States and cities adopt the code, often with their own amendments, and the wording that matters is the version adopted where you live. North Carolina's adopted residential code, for example, requires "all interior egress doors and a minimum of one exterior egress door" to be readily openable from the egress side without a key. A 2023 interpretation from the NC State Fire Marshal's office confirms only one code-compliant exterior egress door is required.

Under wording like that, a keyed interior on the required exit door is a problem, while a secondary exterior door may be treated differently. Elsewhere the text may read more broadly. Minnesota, as another example, adopts the 2018 IRC with amendments of its own.

### Storefronts and offices play by commercial rules

Businesses fall under different provisions. The 2024 International Building Code, section 1010.2.4, allows key-operated locks on the egress side of the main door in certain occupancies only if it is the main exterior or tenant door, it is readily distinguishable as locked, and a sign reading "THIS DOOR TO REMAIN UNLOCKED WHEN THIS SPACE IS OCCUPIED" in 1-inch letters is posted on or next to it.

The building official can revoke this for due cause.

NFPA 101 sets similar conditions and adds that a key must be immediately available to anyone inside while the door is locked. Our page on [deadbolt locks for commercial doors](/commercial-deadbolt-locks/) covers business hardware in more depth.

## Hardware, door prep and keying

Most residential double cylinder deadbolts are made for the same door prep as single cylinder models. Schlage's B-series instructions, for example, call for a 2-1/8" cross bore, a 1" edge bore, a 2-3/8" or 2-3/4" backset and a door 1-3/8" to 1-3/4" thick.

That means converting a door that already has a deadbolt is normally a swap in the existing holes, much like any other deadbolt replacement on a prepped door. We still check the box against your door before we start, because some doors are thicker than the standard range and some models use their own prep.

Heavier hardware exists if you want it. Schlage's commercial B600 series is rated ANSI/BHMA A156.36 Grade 1 and includes a double cylinder version, the B662. On the keying side, both cylinders normally take one key, and if your other locks share a keyway we can match them so the inside key is simply your house key. Our page on [keying every door to one key](/keyed-alike-deadbolts/) explains when that works.

Price depends mainly on the hardware grade you choose, whether the door needs any new prep, and how many locks are keyed together at the same visit. The [deadbolt installation cost guide](/deadbolt-guides/deadbolt-installation-cost/) explains how those factors show up in a quote.

## A key storage plan that works half asleep

The inside key is the part of the job people underestimate. A double cylinder lock is only as safe as the household's ability to find that key without thinking.

<Checklist title="Key storage plan we agree before leaving" items={[
  'One fixed spot for the inside key, on the hinge side of the door or another place no arm through the glass can reach',
  'A spare on every adult\'s and older teen\'s key ring, so a missing hook key is never a dead end',
  'Everyone who stays over, including sitters and visiting relatives, shown the spot on their first visit',
  'The key goes back on the hook every time, not into a pocket or a drawer',
  'Never leave the key sitting in the inside cylinder',
]} />

That last point matters more than it sounds. A key left in the inside cylinder turns the lock back into a thumb turn, the exact thing the double cylinder was meant to remove.

## Alternatives when a second cylinder is the wrong fit

If the code check or the household rules out a keyed interior, the glass is still the weak point. These are the options we talk through instead.

| Option | What it addresses | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| Single cylinder deadbolt with a reinforced strike | Kick-ins at the jamb, with an easy exit kept | The thumb turn is still reachable through the glass |
| Security film on the glass | Slows someone trying to break through the pane | Results depend on the film and the quality of the install |
| Stronger glazing or a solid panel | Removes or toughens the weak point itself | A glazier or door job, not a lock job |
| Removable (captive) thumb turn deadbolt | Turn can be taken off when the home is empty | Ask your AHJ whether removing it counts as special knowledge |
| Keypad or smart deadbolt | Keyless entry from outside | Still has a thumb turn inside, so the glass issue remains |

If keyless entry is the real goal, our smart deadbolt installation service covers the options, but pair it with one of the glass fixes above.

## Field tip: run a lights-off drill before you commit

Before booking, try this at night. Turn off the lights, have each person in the home start from their bed, and ask them to walk to the door and open it using the key from where you plan to hang it.

If anyone hesitates, fumbles or forgets where the key lives, solve that first: move the hook, add a night light, or choose one of the alternatives.
