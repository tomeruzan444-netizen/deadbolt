If a piece of the key sticks out of the cylinder face, one straight, gentle pull with needle-nose pliers is reasonable. Grip it, pull outward without turning, and stop if it doesn't move. Anything flush with the face or deeper needs proper tools.

## What not to put into a jammed keyway

People search for quick tricks when a broken key in deadbolt cylinders strands them at the door. Most of those tricks make the job bigger.

### Superglue on a matchstick or the broken half

This is the one we see most often, and the one that causes the most damage. Glue wicks into the keyway and the pin chambers, sets the pins in place and bonds the fragment to the plug.

Schlage's service manual treats superglue inside a lock as a fault in its own right. Clearing it takes heat or a solvent, both of which carry risks: heat can weaken the springs, and solvents can strip the finish. In severe cases, the manual says, the cylinder has to be replaced.

### Forcing the plug with the broken stub

Turning the stub with pliers can shear the fragment again, leaving a shorter piece deeper in the lock. It can also rotate the plug part way, where the pin chambers no longer line up and the fragment is pinched in place.

### Pushing the other half in alongside it

The broken bow end will not "push the piece through." It drives the fragment further back and adds a second set of scratches to the keyway.

### Jewelry tools, hairpins and saw blades

Improvised probes are thicker and softer than real extractors. They tend to snap off themselves, and now there are two objects in the lock.

<Callout type="warning" title="Leave the lubricant on the shelf for now">
Spraying oil into the keyway can make the fragment harder to grip, and Schlage's manual warns that some chemicals can damage the lock's finish. If the cylinder needs cleaning and lubrication, we do it after extraction with a product suited to that lock.
</Callout>

## How extraction works for a broken key in deadbolt locks

Extraction itself is unglamorous. Schlage's own service manual describes the approach in one line: use a broken key extractor or small forceps to remove the object.

In practice, the plug first has to sit in its home position, the angle at which a key goes in and comes out. That is when the pins release the key's cuts. We then slide a very thin extractor along the side of the fragment, catches the cuts or the blade edge, and draws the piece straight out along the keyway.

When a broken key in deadbolt locks leaves the door locked, the job becomes a lockout too. At Deadbolt Fixers, we confirm who you are and that you have the right to enter before we open anything, exactly as we would on our [deadbolt lockout service](/deadbolt-lockout-service/). With the fragment out, the plug turns, the bolt retracts and you're in.

If the cylinder will not give up the fragment, it can be removed from the door and worked on at the bench. That is still usually cheaper than a new lock.

## When the cylinder itself is damaged

Most locks come through an extraction working normally. Sometimes they don't, and the clues are usually there before the key broke.

| What you notice after extraction | What it usually means | What we do |
|---|---|---|
| The lock works smoothly with a new key | The key failed, not the lock | Cut a new key to code and test |
| The new key works but feels stiff or gritty | Debris, wear or packed lubricant in the cylinder | Clean and relubricate, or repair the cylinder |
| The key was hard to turn for months before it broke | Worn cylinder | Replace the cylinder; Schlage lists replacement as the fix for a key that has grown hard to turn with use |
| Glue, chewed metal or a bent plug face | Cylinder damage from removal attempts | Replace the cylinder, keyed to your existing key if you like |
| The bolt sticks even with the door open | The problem is in the bolt mechanism, not the key | Quote a repair or replacement of the deadbolt |

## Why keys break, and how to stop it happening again

A broken key in deadbolt locks is rarely bad luck. Keys rarely snap from one bad turn. They crack slowly from years of flexing, often near the shoulder where the blade meets the bow, and then fail on an ordinary Tuesday.

Schlage's service manual lists the usual suspects behind a key that stops working well:

- **Wear.** A key worn past factory specification no longer lifts the pins cleanly, so it takes more force.
- **Copies of copies.** A "multi-generation duplicate" picks up small errors with every copy.
- **Poor cutting.** Burrs and wrong angles from a dull key-cutting wheel make a key bind.

Schlage's rekeying guide adds that duplicate keys wear faster than originals. The fix the manual gives is to recut the key to its code, or order a factory-original key.

A stiff lock speeds all of this up. So does using the key as a handle to pull a heavy door closed, or hanging a heavy key ring from it while it sits in the lock.

## Field tip: retire keys before they retire themselves

Hold your most-used key next to the spare. If its cuts look rounded, its tip is worn thin or you can see a hairline crack near the shoulder, have a new key cut from the original code now, not from the worn one. It costs a fraction of an extraction, and it takes the most common cause of a snapped key off your ring.
