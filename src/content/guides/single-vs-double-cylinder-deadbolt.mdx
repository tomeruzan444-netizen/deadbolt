## More keyholes does not mean more security

The most common assumption Deadbolt Fixers hears from homeowners is that a lock with a key on both sides must be the stronger lock. Usually it is not. Within a product line, the double cylinder version typically shares its bolt, housing and outside cylinder with the single cylinder model. Schlage's commercial B600 series, for instance, lists both its single and double cylinder functions under the same Grade 1 rating.

What changes is the inside. A single cylinder deadbolt has a thumb turn that anyone indoors can twist. A double cylinder deadbolt swaps that turn for a second keyed cylinder, so the bolt only moves with a key from either side.

That one difference matters against exactly one kind of break-in, and it creates exactly one new risk. The rest of the single vs double cylinder deadbolt decision is about weighing those two things for your door and your household.

## How each design holds up against real threats

It helps to stop thinking about "secure" in general and look at each way a locked door actually gets opened.

### Reaching through glass

This is the only case where the double cylinder clearly wins. If a glass panel, sidelight or window puts the thumb turn within arm's reach, breaking one pane is enough to open a single cylinder lock. With a keyed interior there is nothing to grab.

### Kicking or shouldering the door

Neither design helps here. Doors that fail under a kick usually fail at the strike and jamb, where short screws hold the strike to thin trim. Consumer Reports found that adding a box strike with 3-inch screws significantly improved kick-in resistance for most locks it tested. That upgrade is covered on our page on [strengthening the strike and jamb](/deadbolt-strike-plate-reinforcement/).

### Picking, bumping or drilling the outside cylinder

The outside cylinder is usually the same on both versions, so the design choice changes nothing. Resistance to these attacks comes from the cylinder itself. If that worries you, look at a [high security deadbolt with a UL 437 listed cylinder](/high-security-deadbolt/) rather than a second cylinder.

### Getting out in an emergency

This is where the single cylinder clearly wins. A thumb turn works by feel, in the dark, in smoke, for a child or a visitor. A double cylinder works only if the right key is in the right place and the person can use it under stress.

## Household-by-household advice

The right lock depends as much on who lives behind the door as on the door itself. Here is how the single vs double cylinder deadbolt question usually plays out for different households.

### Homes with babies, toddlers and school-age kids

Use a single cylinder lock on the main exit door. Some parents like a double cylinder because a toddler cannot open it, but in a fire it also traps older children who could otherwise get themselves out, and it sends the adults looking for a key.

If wandering is the worry, deal with it separately, with measures that slow a small child without locking the adults in. Revisit the choice as children grow; a ten-year-old can learn a thumb turn in one try.

### Older adults and anyone with limited grip or eyesight

Keep the thumb turn. Fishing a key off a hook and turning a cylinder takes fine motor control, good light and a calm head, all of which can be in short supply during an emergency.

The ADA's rule for operable parts in public buildings is a useful benchmark at home too: hardware should work with one hand, without tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist. A large, easy thumb turn meets that idea far better than a small key does.

### Rentals and short-term stays

Landlords face a specific problem: tenants, their children and their guests change, and none of them chose the lock. A keyed interior depends on every one of those people learning where the inside key lives.

Many landlords settle on single cylinder deadbolts and put the effort into a stronger strike instead. If you are considering a double cylinder in a rental, confirm the local code and landlord-tenant rules first.

### Doors with glass panels or sidelights

This is the household where the double cylinder argument is strongest. Measure the problem honestly: stand outside, imagine one pane gone, and check whether your hand would reach the turn.

If it would, and the door is not the home's main exit, a double cylinder lock is a reasonable choice for a household that can manage the key. If the door is the main exit, look at glass upgrades or security film first and keep the thumb turn.

### Vacation homes and shared houses

Guests and housemates are the least likely people to know where an inside key is kept. A house that changes occupants often is a poor fit for a keyed interior, even if the glass would justify one.

Smart and keypad deadbolts do not change this decision. They still have a thumb turn inside, and Consumer Reports notes that smart locks are as susceptible to forced entry as conventional ones. Glass near a keypad lock is the same weak point as glass near any single cylinder model.
</Callout>

## What building codes say, in plain terms

Residential codes in many US states are based on the International Residential Code. Its egress door rule, quoted from the 2009 model edition, says egress doors "shall be readily openable from inside the dwelling without the use of a key or special knowledge or effort." A double cylinder deadbolt needs a key, so it is the obvious thing that rule is aimed at.

The model code is not the law until a state or city adopts it, often with amendments. North Carolina's adopted version, for example, applies the keyless rule to interior egress doors and at least one exterior egress door. Minnesota adopts the 2018 IRC with its own amendments. The result is that two neighboring towns can treat the same back door differently.

Businesses follow separate commercial provisions in the International Building Code and NFPA 101, which allow key-operated locks on some main doors only under strict conditions such as signage. None of this makes double cylinder locks "illegal" across the board, but it does mean the local building department, the authority having jurisdiction, decides what is allowed on your door.

<Callout type="code">
Before buying a double cylinder deadbolt, call your local building department, tell them which door it is for and whether the home has another exterior door, and ask whether a lock that needs a key from inside is acceptable there.
</Callout>

## Mixing both types in one house

You do not have to settle the single vs double cylinder deadbolt question once for the whole house. A common and sensible layout looks like this:

- **Main exit door:** single cylinder deadbolt with a reinforced strike, so everyone has one guaranteed keyless way out.
- **Secondary door with glass beside the lock:** double cylinder, if the local code allows it and the household passes the key test.
- **Other exterior doors without nearby glass:** single cylinder.

Whatever the mix, put every cylinder on one key. A double cylinder lock is much easier to live with when the inside key is the same key everyone already carries, which our page on [keyed alike deadbolts](/keyed-alike-deadbolts/) explains.

Grade matters more than cylinder count for overall strength. If you are comparing models, our [explanation of ANSI/BHMA deadbolt grades](/deadbolt-guides/deadbolt-grades-ansi-bhma/) shows how to read the ratings on the box, and good locks come in both versions.

## The two-question decision rule

When the single vs double cylinder deadbolt choice still feels close, reduce it to two questions: whether someone outside could reach the thumb turn through glass, and whether this is the door your family would use to get out in a fire.

If the first answer is no, or the second is yes, choose a single cylinder. Only a reachable turn on a secondary door makes a double cylinder worth the trade.
