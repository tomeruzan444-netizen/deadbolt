## Three questions that sort every deadbolt

A deadbolt is a lock with a bolt that only moves when someone deliberately turns it. Schlage's training material puts it plainly: a deadbolt cannot lock itself. That single trait is what separates it from the spring latch in a knob or lever, which snaps shut on its own when the door closes.

Everything else about the many types of deadbolts comes down to three questions:

- **Where are the keyholes?** Key outside with a thumb turn inside (single cylinder), or a key on both sides (double cylinder).
- **How is it mounted?** Bored through the door, screwed to the inside surface (rim), or set into a deep pocket in the door edge (mortise).
- **How is it operated?** By key only, by keypad code, or by a smart lock that talks to your phone or home network.

A keypad deadbolt can also be a single cylinder bored lock. A commercial mortise deadbolt can also be double cylinder. Once you see the categories as separate choices, product names stop being confusing.

Here is what each type changes:

- **Double cylinder:** replaces the thumb turn (5) with a second keyed cylinder.
- **Keypad and smart:** replace the exterior cylinder (2) with a keypad or add electronics, and often add a motor to turn the bolt.
- **Rim:** moves the bolt (3) out of the door edge and onto the inside surface of the door.
- **Mortise:** replaces the small bolt assembly (3) with a box-shaped lock body set deep into the door edge.
- **High security:** upgrades the cylinder (2) with a pick- and drill-resistant design.

## Single cylinder deadbolts

This is the default exterior door lock in the US: a key on the outside, a thumb turn on the inside. Anyone inside can get out with a quick turn, which is why most people never think about it.

Schlage's B60 is a typical example. It has a 1 inch bolt, fits a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset, and comes with a strike reinforcer that uses 3 inch screws. Most mainstream brands follow the same door prep, so one single cylinder bored lock can usually replace another without new drilling.

If your door has no deadbolt at all, a [new deadbolt installation](/deadbolt-lock-installation/) means boring those holes from scratch. If one is already there and worn out or outdated, [replacing the existing deadbolt](/deadbolt-replacement/) is the simpler job.

## Double cylinder deadbolts

A double cylinder deadbolt has a keyed cylinder on both sides of the door. No thumb turn, so nobody can open it from inside without a key. Commercial lines treat it as a separate function: Schlage's B600 series, for example, lists the B660 single cylinder and the B662 double cylinder as different models.

People usually consider one for a door with glass close to the lock. The catch is getting out in a fire. Building codes generally require the egress door of a home to open from inside without a key, but codes are adopted locally, and the local building official decides what applies to your door.

The pros, cons and code questions get a full treatment in our [single vs double cylinder comparison](/deadbolt-guides/single-vs-double-cylinder-deadbolt/). If you have already decided, see how we handle [double cylinder deadbolt installation](/double-cylinder-deadbolt-installation/), including the key-storage plan.

## Keypad and keyless deadbolts

A keyless deadbolt lets you in with a code instead of a key. Keypad deadbolts come in two broad styles, and the difference matters on a door that swells or shrinks with the seasons.

- **Manual-throw keypads.** The code releases the lock, and you turn the bolt by hand. Schlage once explained that its keypad deadbolt worked this way so the bolt would lock securely even as doors expand and contract with temperature and humidity.
- **Motorized keypads.** A motor drives the bolt after the code is accepted. If the bolt hits the strike, the lock reports a jam, and Schlage names misalignment as a common cause.

Batteries are the other thing to plan for. Yale's keypad deadbolt runs on four AA cells and warns with a steady red light and five beeps when they are low. Its instructions say to use the mechanical key if the lock fails, which is why the backup key belongs somewhere you can reach from outside.

## Smart deadbolts

A smart deadbolt is a keypad or motorized lock that also connects to something: your phone, a voice assistant or a home automation hub. The Schlage Encode Plus, for example, has built-in Wi-Fi on a 2.4 GHz network, works with Apple Home, Alexa and Google Assistant, and runs on four AA batteries with a backup key.

Smart locks connect over Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Z-Wave, Zigbee or Matter. The Connectivity Standards Alliance describes Thread, one of Matter's networks, as best suited to low-power devices such as locks.

There are two ways to make a deadbolt smart:

- **Full replacement:** the whole lock is swapped for a smart deadbolt with its own bolt and cylinder.
- **Retrofit:** a smart unit is added to the deadbolt already on the door. Consumer Reports notes that a retrofit's resistance to kicking, picking and drilling depends entirely on that existing deadbolt.

Either way, a motorized bolt needs a door that closes square, because the motor cannot force a bolt past a misaligned strike. Our [smart deadbolt installation service](/smart-deadbolt-installation/) at Deadbolt Fixers checks alignment before the app setup.

## Bored, rim and mortise: how the lock mounts

Of all the ways to sort the types of deadbolts, mounting is the one most buyers overlook. It decides what can replace the lock without carpentry, and it is the hardest thing to change later.

### Bored (tubular) deadbolts

The bored lock, also called a tubular deadbolt, is the standard on residential doors. Schlage's B-Series instructions call for a 2-1/8 inch cross bore through the door face (1-1/2 inches on some models) and a 1 inch edge bore, with a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset.

Fine Homebuilding points out that the tubular bolt is neither spring-loaded nor beveled, and installation needs two holes drilled plus two shallow mortises: one for the faceplate on the door edge and one for the strike in the frame. That faceplate is small. Kwikset's 660 spec lists it at 1 inch by 2-1/4 inches.

### Rim deadbolts

A rim lock is surface-mounted on the inside face of the door. Only the key cylinder passes through the door to the outside, so the exterior looks much like any other keyed deadbolt.

Because the lock body is not buried in the door edge, there is no faceplate visible on the edge of the door. That makes a rim lock easy to spot.

### Mortise deadbolts

A mortise lock sits in a deep rectangular pocket cut into the door edge. Schlage calls mortise locks "the most secure type of lock" and notes that a mortise unit can combine a latch, a deadlocking latch and a deadbolt in one body, with the key cylinder threaded into a standardized housing.

Schlage's L400 small-case mortise deadbolt shows the style: a 1 inch stainless steel bolt, a 2-3/4 inch backset only, a 1-3/4 inch standard door, and a UL listing for 3-hour fire doors. Schlage suggests it for doors that do not need a latch, multi-stall restroom entrances and utility rooms.

## Commercial and specialty deadbolts

Business doors add a few types of deadbolts you rarely see on houses. These all belong to the same family, but each solves a specific problem.

- **Interconnected locks** combine a lever lock and a deadbolt so one turn of the inside lever pulls back both. Schlage describes this as single-motion egress.
- **Interchangeable core deadbolts** let the key core slide out with a special control key. Schlage's B600 deadbolt offers full-size and small-format interchangeable core cylinders, and its service manual describes the control key working at a separate shear line.
- **Function-specific models.** The B600 line also lists a classroom deadbolt function and a door bolt function alongside the single and double cylinder versions.

Choosing between these depends on staffing, key control and the door itself. Our page on [deadbolts for commercial doors](/commercial-deadbolt-locks/) covers storefront and mortise work.

## High security deadbolts

A high security deadbolt is usually a standard body with a far better cylinder. Schlage's Primus cylinders are UL 437 listed and use hardened steel, anti-drill pins and a second sidebar mechanism, with keys that are tightly controlled to make unauthorized copies hard.

The cylinder is often the whole difference. Schlage's commercial B600 deadbolt, for instance, is UL 437 listed only when fitted with a Primus UL 437 cylinder. If you are weighing that upgrade, see what goes into a [pick- and drill-resistant high security deadbolt](/high-security-deadbolt/).

## Types of deadbolts compared side by side

This table puts the common types of deadbolts in one place. Remember that mounting and operation are separate choices, so a real lock can sit in more than one row.

| Type | Outside | Inside | Where you see it | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single cylinder | Key | Thumb turn | Most home entry doors | A short-screw strike that only catches the jamb |
| Double cylinder | Key | Key | Doors with glass near the lock | Egress rules; a key must be on hand inside |
| Keypad, manual throw | Code (key backup) | Thumb turn | Rentals, side and garage entry doors | Batteries |
| Keypad, motorized | Code (key backup) | Thumb turn | Entry doors where hands are full | Strike alignment and batteries |
| Smart, full replacement | Code, app or key | Thumb turn | Homes with a smart home system | Network setup, batteries |
| Smart, retrofit | Your existing key | Motorized turn unit | Renters and owners keeping their lock | Only as strong as the existing deadbolt |
| Bored (tubular) | Varies | Varies | Nearly all residential doors | Backset must match: 2-3/8" or 2-3/4" |
| Rim | Key | Thumb turn | Mounted on the inside face of the door | Lock body is exposed on the inside |
| Mortise | Key | Thumb turn or key | Commercial doors, restrooms, utility rooms | Needs a deep pocket in the door edge |
| Interconnected | Key | Lever retracts both | Commercial doors | Replacing it means replacing both locks |
| High security cylinder | Restricted key | Thumb turn or key | Any door that needs key control | Keys come only from authorized sources |

## Where grades fit in

Any of these types can be built to a higher or lower rating. Residential deadbolts are rated under ANSI/BHMA A156.40 with letters A, B and C, and many also carry a Grade 1, 2 or 3 under a separate auxiliary lock standard.

The type tells you how a lock works. The rating tells you how well it was tested. Our guide to [what ANSI/BHMA deadbolt grades mean](/deadbolt-guides/deadbolt-grades-ansi-bhma/) shows how to read both on the box.
